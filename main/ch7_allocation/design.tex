\section{Design of dynamic control allocators}
\label{sec:all_design}

The results that follow in the rest of this chapter provide solutions to \Cref{prb:ssinv,prb:fullinv} based on different configurations of \eqref{eq:bbsys}--\eqref{eq:bbexosys}. The case of \Cref{prb:ssinv} is examined considering both a linear and a nonlinear model for the system \eqref{eq:bbsys}. On the other hand, \Cref{prb:fullinv} is studied from the only point of view of linear systems but in multiple scenarios: when the model of $\bbsys$ is fully known, when it is affected by parametric uncertainties, and when it includes sampled data devices.

\subsection{Dynamic allocation with steady-state output invisibility}
\label{sec:all_design_ssinv}
The case identified by \Cref{prb:ssinv} where the systems \eqref{eq:bbsys}--\eqref{eq:bbexosys} are linear has been studied in the literature and multiple solutions have been proposed, as reported in \Cref{sec:sota_allocation}. However, none of these solutions retain their applicability when the model exhibits less convenient features, which is one of the reasons why the dynamic control allocation problem for nonlinear systems is still an open issue. This section presents the work that has been done in this direction, starting from the following motivating idea. Since, at its core, the dynamic control allocation problem is a constrained dynamic optimization problem, it is beneficial to reformulate it as such. This allows one to use approaches and tools belonging to the theory of optimal control.

\subsection{Dynamic allocation with full output invisibility}
\label{sec:all_design_fullinv}
The discussion of \Cref{prb:ssinv} in \Cref{sec:all_design_ssinv} has highlighted a common aspect of all dynamic control allocation problems: the invisibility requirement, formulated either as a problem statement or as an optimization constraint, is the hardest one to manage. The steady-state formulation of \Cref{prb:ssinv} allows the designer to exploit the steady-state behavior of the system, once it is determined, to design the dynamics of the allocator. This is not possible when dealing with \Cref{prb:fullinv}, which requires to account for the full dynamics of \eqref{eq:bbsys}--\eqref{eq:bbexosys}.

The intuition at the heart of the design of dynamic allocators achieving full output invisibility is illustrated in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014}. In essence, given items (I) and (O) of \Cref{prb:fullinv}, the allocator dynamics have to be split into multiple subsystems, each of which deals with one of the two requirements. While \cite{cristofaro_output_2014} deals with a problem similar to \Cref{prb:fullinv} but in which a steady-state cost \emph{function} is considered, its results provide a way to develop an approach suitable for \Cref{prb:fullinv}. An allocator solving \Cref{prb:fullinv} is composed by, at least, the interconnection of the following two subsystems:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an \emph{annihilator}, responsible for the full output invisibility requirement and acting as a filter that masks the effect of the allocation action on the regulated output;
  \item an \emph{optimizer}, responsible for the optimality requirement and acting as a dynamic controller that computes the allocation action to bring the cost functional \eqref{eq:bbcost} to its minimal value;
  \item a \emph{steady-state generator}, which contains an internal model of the exosystem \eqref{eq:bbexosys} and is in charge of producing functions of time within a prescribed set.
\end{itemize}
The interconnection of these three basic components is depicted in \Cref{fig:fullallocator}. Note that, when the considered steady-state is constant, a possible implementation of the optimizer is based on the gradient descent method \cite{khalil_nonlinear_2002} and the steady-state generator is naturally embedded within its integrator, thus reducing the architecture to only two distinct components instead of three.

As highlighted in \cite[Remark 4]{cristofaro_output_2014}, this construction allows the designer to overcome cumbersome limitations encountered in earlier works that attempt to achieve full output invisibility but fail to do so without relying on quite non-trivial properties of $\bbsys$; an example of this for linear systems is found in \cite{zaccarian_dynamic_2009} with the \emph{strong input redundancy} property. Another important aspect of the allocator proposed in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014} is that the design of its three components is carried out in sequence, as summarized in the following steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The annihilator is designed first, exploiting the dynamic model of the system $\bbsys$ and its properties. The idea is to design a system whose input-output behavior is \emph{orthogonal} to that of the system $\bbsys$, with respect to the regulated output $\bbouto$. The results in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014} provide two different ways to design such a component: a polynomial method to compute orthogonal transfer matrices, and a geometric approach based on invariant subspaces.
  \item The optimizer is designed next, using a static description of the steady-state behavior of the annihilator that is now available as, \emph{e.g.}, a static gain (see again \cite{astolfi_model_2010,scarciotti_data-driven_2017}). The design of this component is aimed at minimizing the steady-state cost in \eqref{eq:bbcost}, so it mainly depends on the formulation of the cost itself.
  \item The steady-state generator is designed last, integrating an internal model of the exosystem $\bbexo$.
\end{enumerate}

This work focuses on the design of an allocator solving \Cref{prb:fullinv} in the case of a linear model of the form of \eqref{eq:bbsys}--\eqref{eq:bbexosys} and in two different situations: when the model is fully known and when it is affected by parametric uncertainties. In particular, the design of the annihilator is carried out applying a polynomial approach that is derived from the one discussed in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014} but that is then adapted to the uncertain case. Since the two methods share many similarities, especially in the nominal case, an overview of the original method presented in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014} is omitted; the interested reader is referred to the original work for more insights on the problem formulation thereby considered, as well as on the geometric annihilator design approach.

\subsection{Sampled-data dynamic allocation with full output invisibility}
\label{sec:all_design_sd}
The previous results show how the design of dynamic allocators achieving full output invisibility for linear systems can be carried out in a systematic way, by splitting the allocator into three subsystems that are designed in sequence. Moreover, it has also been shown how the data required to design these subsystems can be estimated online from sampled measurements of the inputs and outputs of a preexisting control system. In practice, it is reasonable to expect that system \eqref{eq:bbsys} includes digital devices, \emph{e.g.}, microcontrollers, as part of the control loop to implement the given control laws. Moreover, it is also reasonable to expect that the allocator itself, together with its estimation algorithms (if required), is to be implemented on a digital device connected to the control system. In this regard, some considerations have been made in \Cref{rem:ddall_nablaG_comp} but the behavior of an input allocation device of the form shown in \Cref{fig:fullallocator} implemented on a digital device remains an unexplored topic; the majority of the available literature on the subject is focused on the continuous-time design of dynamic allocators (see \Cref{sec:sota_allocation}). The first question to address is whether one has to expect a different behavior when sampled-data devices are involved.
