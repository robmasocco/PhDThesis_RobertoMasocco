\section{Design of a dynamic current allocator for the TCV shape controller}
\label{sec:tcvall}
The design of a dynamic coil current allocator for the TCV shape controller requires an inspection of the preexisting control scheme, to clarify which among the novel formulations presented in \Cref{sec:all_design} is the most appropriate. Subsequently, the selected solution is adapted to the specific case of the TCV shape controller, accounting for any differences arising with respect to the previously analyzed scenarios.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{sec:tcvall_problem}
Consider the simplified control scheme given in \Cref{fig:tcvcsh}, which summarizes the interconnection of the main control subsystems with the TCV plant. Before going further, it is instrumental to anticipate that every dynamic model considered in the following is continuous-time, linear time-invariant; this is possible thanks to the powerful real-time equilibrium reconstruction codes that are part of the SCD control suite, which allow control engineers to work on a linearized model of the plant around the prescribed shape equilibrium for every shot (see \Cref{sec:tcv,sec:scd,sec:sota_tcvshapectrl}).
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=\linewidth]{images/ch7/tcvcsh.svg}
  \caption{Simplified diagram of the TCV control scheme: the $P_h$ block represents the actual plant, interconnected to the \emph{hybrid} feedback controller $C_h$ and to the shape controller, represented by the $C_{sh}$ block.}
  \label{fig:tcvcsh}
\end{figure}

The central block $P_h$ represents the dynamics of the plasma and coils, described by the following equations:
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tcv_ph}
  \begin{align}
    \dot{x}_{ph}(t) &= A_{ph}x_{ph}(t) + B_{ph}u_{h}(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},\\
    y(t) &= C_{ph}x_{ph}(t) + D_{ph}u_{h}(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $x_{ph}(t)\in\R^{275}$ is the state vector at time $t$, containing the currents in the active circuits $I_a(t)\in\R^{19}$ and in the filaments used to represent the vessel, namely $I_u(t)\in\R^{256}$; $u_{h}(t)\in\R^{19}$ is the plant input, containing the voltages $V_a(t)\in\R^{19}$ to be applied to the active circuits; $y(t) = \begin{bmatrix}y_h(t)' & y_{sh}(t)' & I_{EF}(t)'\end{bmatrix}'$ is the plant output, containing the controlled variables $y_h(t)\in\R^{24}$, namely the plasma current and its radial and vertical positions as well as the currents of the PF coils, the shape output $y_{sh}(t)\in\R^{q_{sh}}$ whose dimension depends on the design of the shape controller for the desired equilibrium, and the measurements $I_{EF}(t)\in\R^{16}$ of the currents of the EF coils.

The inner control loop comprises also the feedback controller $C_h$, informally termed \emph{hybrid} due to its composition of both analog and digital devices \cite{lister_control_1997}. The role of $C_h$ is to compute the active voltages $V_a$ which are to be applied to the PF coils to track the reference input $r_h$, taking care of the vertical and radial stabilization of the plasma and controlling the current flow in the PF coils. Its state-space representation is given by
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tcv_ch}
  \begin{align}
    \dot{x}_{ch}(t) &= A_{ch}x_{ch}(t) + B_{ch}e_h(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},\\
    u_h(t) &= C_{ch}x_{ch}(t) + D_{ch}e_h(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $x_{ch}(t)\in\R^{n_{ch}}$ is the state vector of the hybrid controller at time $t$, $e_h(t)\in\R^{24}$ is the hybrid error, $e_h = r_h - y_h$, and $u_h(t)\in\R^{19}$ is the hybrid controller output.

The shape controller is embedded in the $C_{sh}$ block and its state-space representation is
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tcv_csh}
  \begin{align}
    \dot{x}_{csh}(t) &= A_{csh}x_{csh}(t) + B_{csh}e_{sh}(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},\\
    u_{sh}(t) &= C_{csh}x_{csh}(t) + D_{csh}e_{sh}(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $x_{csh}(t)\in\R^{n_{csh}}$ is the state vector of the shape controller at time $t$, $e_{sh}(t)\in\R^{q_{sh}}$ is the shape error, $e_{sh} = r_{sh} - y_{sh}$, and $u_{sh}(t)\in\R^{24}$ is the shape controller output, namely the variations to be applied to modify the prescribed feedforward references $r_{h,ff}$ for the hybrid controller, which are precomputed for each shot.

\begin{assumption}
  \label{ass:tcvstab}
  The closed-loop system \eqref{eq:tcv_ph}--\eqref{eq:tcv_csh}, interconnected as in
  \begin{align}
    \label{eq:tcv_interconn}
    e_{sh} &= r_{sh} - y_{sh}, & r_h &= r_{h,ff} + u_{sh},
  \end{align}
  is well-posed and aymptotically stable.\closestatement
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}
  \label{ass:tcvoveract}
  $q_{sh} < 24$.\closestatement
\end{assumption}

In practice, the validity of \Cref{ass:tcvoveract} (that is analogous to \Cref{ass:bboveract}) depends on the design of the shape controller, \emph{i.e.}, on the number $q_{sh}$ of shape outputs that are considered to track the desired shape. This is a tunable parameter of the TCV shape controller \cite{mele_design_2024}, because it depends on the number of control points that define the desired shape. Thus, if the control objective for a given experiment is suitable, the designer can reduce the number of regulated outputs and exploit TCV's redundant array of coils to improve the performance of the control system.

\begin{assumption}
  \label{ass:tcvrefs}
  The shape reference signal $r_{sh}$ is such that
  \begin{align}
    \label{eq:tcvrefs}
    r_{sh}(t) &= \bar{r}_{sh}, & t\in\R_{\geq 0},
  \end{align}
  with $\bar{r}_{sh}\in\R^{q_{sh}}$.\closestatement
\end{assumption}

It is worth to point out that even if \Cref{ass:tcvrefs} holds, which in other words implies that a desired shape to track is encoded as a set of $q_{sh}$ constant scalar references, the resulting steady-state reference for the hybrid controller $C_h$, namely $\tilde{r}_h$, may not be a constant signal; this is due to the precomputed hybrid reference $r_{h,ff}$ that may include ramps. However, for the sake of simplicity, the design of the allocator can be carried out discarding the presence of the feedforward term $r_{h,ff}$. Thus, in accordance with the analysis carried out in \Cref{sec:all_design}, \Cref{ass:tcvrefs} implies that the steady-state behavior of the closed loop is not periodic. Hence, the steady-state cost functional \eqref{eq:bbcost} reduces to the \emph{steady-state cost function}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tcvcost}
  J = \ell(\tilde{I}_{EF}),
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{I}_{EF}$ is the steady-state value of the EF coil currents, and the function $\ell : \R^{16} \rightarrow \R$ is at least locally convex. A specific choice of the performance criterion with respect to which the steady-state behavior of the EF coil currents must be optimized yields a specific form of the function $\ell$. The dynamic EF coil current allocation problem for the TCV tokamak can be formulated as follows.

\begin{problem}[Dynamic EF coil current allocation for TCV shape control]
  \label{prb:tcvall}
  Consider the TCV feedback control system \eqref{eq:tcv_ph}--\eqref{eq:tcv_ch} with the shape controller \eqref{eq:tcv_csh} and suppose that \Cref{ass:tcvstab,ass:tcvoveract,ass:tcvrefs} hold.
  Find, if any, an input allocation device (briefly, an \emph{allocator}) having input $\allin$ and output $\allout$ such that, when interconnected according to
  \begin{align}
    \label{eq:tcv_all_interconn}
    \allin &= u_{sh} + r_{h,ff}, & r_h &= u_{sh} + r_{h,ff} + \allout,
  \end{align}
  ensures that the following requirements hold.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item[\emph{(I)}] \emph{Full shape output invisibility:} the shape output both in presence and absence of the allocator is such that
      \begin{align}
        \label{eq:tcv_all_inv}
        y_{sh}(t) &= y^a_{sh}(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0}.
      \end{align}
    \item[\emph{(O)}] \emph{Steady-state optimality}: the cost function \eqref{eq:tcvcost} is minimized.
  \end{itemize}
  \closestatement
\end{problem}

\subsection{Design of the dynamic EF current allocator}
\label{sec:tcvall_design}
The statement of \Cref{prb:tcvall} shows many similarities with that of \Cref{prb:fullinv}. Thus, recalling the literature review offered in \Cref{sec:sota_allocation}, a suitable solution to it is found starting from those presented in \Cref{sec:all_design_fullinv}. First, the full shape output invisibility requirement stated in item (I) justifies an approach similar to that introduced in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014}, \emph{i.e.}, the design of an allocator comprising three subsystems each responsible for one of the requirements, such as the dynamic annihilator that deals with the shape output preservation. However, the main difference with respect to all the cases analyzed in \Cref{sec:all_design_fullinv} and in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014} is stated in \Cref{ass:tcvrefs}, \emph{i.e.}, the intended steady-state behavior is not periodic but constant. This implies that the \emph{steady-state generator} component of the architecture introduced in \cite{cristofaro_output_2014} and further developed in this work is not necessary and that the interconnection of the allocator with the preexisting shape control system can be carried out as in \eqref{eq:tcv_all_interconn}. The proposed architecture is depicted in \Cref{fig:tcvall}, whereas the design procedure of both the \emph{dynamic annihilator} and \emph{steady-state optimizer} units is described in the following. After the selection of the most suitable architecture, the next design decision that has been taken concerned the nature of the dynamic systems to be implemented, having to choose among either a continuous-time or a discrete-time design. Even if the TCV control system is now almost entirely composed of sampled-data, digital devices (see \Cref{sec:scd,sec:all_design_fullinv}), the proposed design has been carried out in the continuous time domain. This choice has been made for two technical reasons related to the SCD implementation: first, the design of all the control systems integrated in the SCD is performed in continuous-time, and their discretization is automatically performed by the build system that generates and compiles the real-time control code. Secondly, the sampling rates of the SCD are very high, typically in the order of $\SI{e4}{\hertz}$, thus making negligible any error induced by the discretization in the majority of contexts. The validity of the proposed design follows from the results cited in \Cref{sec:sota_allocation} and from those illustrated in \Cref{sec:all_design_fullinv}.

\subsubsection{Design of the dynamic annihilator}
\label{sec:tcvall_ann}
Let $\bbreddeg = q_{sh} - 24$ be the degree of redundancy of the closed loop system \eqref{eq:tcv_ph}--\eqref{eq:tcv_ch} with respect to the shape output $y_{sh}$. Let $W_{y_{sh}{r_h}}(s)$ be the transfer matrix from the hybrid reference input $r_h$ to the shape output $y_{sh}$, $W_{y_{sh}{r_h}}(s)\in\C^{q_{sh} \times 24}$. Recalling \Cref{sec:all_design_fullinv}, the transfer matrix of the dynamic annihilator is designed as $W_a(s)\in\C^{24 \times \bbreddeg}$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tcvall_ann_cf}
  W_a(s) = N_a(s)d^{-1}(s),
\end{equation}
where $d^{-1}(s)$ is a Hurwitz polynomial of degree larger than that of any element of $N_a(s)$, and $N_a(s)\in\C^{24 \times \bbreddeg}$ is a polynomial annihilator for $W_{y_{sh}{r_h}}(s)$, \emph{i.e.}, it is such that
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:tcvall_ann}
  W_{y_{sh}{r_h}}(s)N_a(s) &= 0, & s\in\C\setminus(\poleset(W_{y_{sh}{r_h}}(s))).
\end{align}
Then, the state-space representation of the annihilator is obtained by a minimal realization $(A_a,B_a,C_a,0)$ of $W_a(s)$, yielding
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tcvall_ann_ss}
  \begin{align}
    \dot{x}_a(t) &= A_a x_a(t) + B_a v(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},\\
    \allout(t) &= C_a x_a(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $x_a(t)$ is the state vector of the annihilator at time $t$, $v(t)\in\R^{\bbreddeg}$ is the input of the annihilator, and $\allout(t)\in\R^{24}$ is the output of the annihilator.

\subsubsection{Design of the steady-state optimizer}
\label{sec:tcvall_ssopt}
Recall \eqref{eq:tcv_all_interconn} and the steady-state cost function \eqref{eq:tcvcost}. Let $P^\star_{I_{EF}}\in\R^{16 \times 24}$ be the static gain of the TCV plant from the input $r_h$ to the output $I_{EF}$, and $P^\star_a\in\R^{24 \times \bbreddeg}$ be the static gain of the dynamic annihilator \eqref{eq:tcvall_ann_ss}. It holds that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tcvall_ssef}
  \tilde{I}_{EF} = P^\star_{I_{EF}}(\allssin + \tilde{\allout}) = P^\star_{I_{EF}}(\allssin + P^\star_a \tilde{v}).
\end{equation}
Thus, let $x_o(t)\in\R^{\bbreddeg}$ for any $t\in\R_{\geq 0}$ and consider the cost function
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tcvall_sscost}
  \tilde{J}(x_o,\allin) = \ell(P^\star_{I_{EF}}(\allin + P^\star_a x_o)),
\end{equation}
with $\ell$ as in \eqref{eq:tcvcost}. Let $\gamma\in\R_{>0}$ and $K\in\R^{\bbreddeg\times\bbreddeg}$ such that $K\succ 0$. According to \cite{zaccarian_dynamic_2009,cristofaro_output_2014}, applying the gradient descent method \cite{khalil_nonlinear_2002} yields the following dynamics for the steady-state optimizer:
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tcvall_ssopt}
  \begin{align}
    \dot{x}_o(t) &= -\gamma K \left.\frac{\partial\tilde{J}(x_o,\allin)}{\partial x_o}\right|_{x_o = x_o(t),\,\allin = \allin(t)}', & t\in\R_{\geq 0},\\
    v(t) &= x_o(t), & t\in\R_{\geq 0},
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\gamma$ is the update step of the gradient descent method, which can be tuned to control the convergence speed of the optimizer, and $K$ further tunes the convergence speed of the single optimization directions in $x_o$.

\subsubsection{Offline design and validation procedure}
\label{sec:tcvall_val}
Further comments regarding the actual implementation of the design procedure for the proposed allocator are in order here. For a given shape equilibrium and shape controller design corresponding to an experiment to be performed, a linearized model is first obtained using the code suites developed at SPC, namely the MATLAB EQuilibrium Toolbox (MEQ) \cite{parys_modeling_2024} and the LIUQE code \cite{moret_tokamak_2015}. The model of the closed loop comprising the TCV plant \eqref{eq:tcv_ph} and the hybrid controller \eqref{eq:tcv_ch} linearized around the shape equilibrium possesses, in general, a high number of states (typically greater than 250). Then, a model reduction is performed applying a balancing transformation \cite{laub_computation_1987} and a principal component analysis \cite{moore_principal_1981}, to ensure that the design procedure of the dynamic annihilator remains numerically feasible; the desired order for the reduced model is a design parameter that can be tuned according to the resulting input-output characteristics of the reduced model, which are compared against those of the original linearized model. This procedure allows the designer to reduce the order of the model down to, in practice, no more than 25 states. The polynomial annihilator \eqref{eq:tcvall_ann} is determined by first computing a left coprime factorization of the transfer matrix $W_{y_{sh}{r_h}}(s)$, which is obtained from the reduced model, and then applying the numerical algorithm described in \cite{tenaglia_robust_2023} to compute the polynomial matrix $N_a(s)$; this procedure, although numerically and computationally intensive, yields a solution to the problem of determining the polynomial annihilator that can be implemented in a straightforward fashion within the SCD code suite. The remaining parameters of the steady-state optimizer \eqref{eq:tcvall_ssopt} are then tuned, and the entire allocator design is then validated, by means of simulations of the shot performed using the MEQ and FBT codes \cite{hofmann_fbt_1988,merle_full_2024,marchioni_vertical_2023,coosemans_application_2024}.
